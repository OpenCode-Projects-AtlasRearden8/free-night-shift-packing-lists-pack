Fatigue-Prep One-Pager

General, widely-known sleep-hygiene and shift-work survival ideas, collected in one place so you can print it and tape it where you decide things. This is education, not medical advice. Sleep problems, chronic fatigue, and anything health-shaped deserve a conversation with your own clinician.

Education only — not medical advice. No diagnosis, treatment, dosing, or clinical guidance appears on this page. If falling asleep, staying awake on a drive, or exhaustion is a recurring problem for you, that is worth talking to your own doctor. And if you are ever too tired to drive safely: stop. Nap somewhere safe, call someone, or take transit. Nothing on a shift is worth a crash.

1. Protect the day-sleep (before the shift block starts)

Tear-out — tape near your bed or blackout curtains

2. Light: use it like a tool (educational summary)

  • Bright light tends to make you more alert; many night workers want it during the first part of the shift and less of it near the end.
  • Sunglasses on the morning drive home are a classic trick precisely because morning sunlight is a wake-up signal to your body clock; your eyes' exposure choices are yours.
  • If light timing, shift-change fatigue, or sleep quality is a persistent struggle, a sleep-medicine clinician is exactly the right person to ask — this page can't help with that and they can.

3. Naps, if naps are possible (rules permitting)

  • Short naps (roughly 10–20 minutes) are the common recommendation for a break-length reset; deep-sleep length naps mid-shift can leave some people groggier.
  • Set an alarm before you close your eyes, not as an aspiration.
  • Know your own grogginess pattern — some people need 10 minutes with water and light after any nap before charting decisions.
  • If your facility doesn't allow break naps, the "pre-shift nap" before your commute is often the more realistic tool anyway.

4. The drive home: make the plan while you're fresh

Tear-out — glovebox card for the too-tired morning

5. Stretch blocks: the weekly version

  • Working 3 in a row then cracking into day-shift world is the classic burnout recipe; if your schedule allows it, protecting one full off-day after a block helps most people.
  • Rotations: some schedules rotate forward (day → evening → night) because it tends to be easier on the body clock than rotating backward; that's a scheduling-team conversation.
  • Track your own data for two weeks (sleep length, how you felt on the drive) and let the paper, not memory, argue with the scheduler for you. Use the locker card or any notebook — this pack ships no app and asks for no logins.

6. When to hand this to a professional

  • Exhaustion that sleep doesn't fix, mood changes, relying on caffeine to function at all — those are clinician conversations, not checklist conversations.
  • Sleep disorders are real, common, treatable, and not a character flaw.
  • Your employee-assistance program or sleep-medicine service may have resources this one-pager can't offer.
